\section{Discussion}

Tree signatures convert heterogeneous gene family trees into vectors indexed by common reference species trees. Their
main practical advantage is a separation between fitting the coordinate system and projecting observations: once the
reference collection is fixed, a new gene tree can be evaluated without recomputing distances among all previously
observed trees. Unlike an opaque generic embedding, every coordinate retains an interpretation as a particular
gene--species tree dissimilarity. The reusable native tree-space interface strengthens this design by avoiding repeated
Newick parsing when the same trees are used across calculations.

The representation is process-agnostic only in a limited sense. It does not fit a joint generative model of gene and
species evolution, but several coordinates are motivated by reconciliation events. A small duplication score, for
example, is evidence of topological compatibility under an LCA mapping; it is not an estimate of a duplication rate or
proof that duplication generated the observed discordance. Methods such as ASTRAL-Pro and SpeciesRax instead optimise a
species-tree objective that explicitly accommodates multi-copy families \citep{astral-pro,Morel2022SpeciesRax}. Tree
signatures are complementary: they are intended for exploration and prediction across gene families rather than as a
replacement for mechanistic species-tree inference.

The reference trees determine the coordinate system and therefore influence distances, projections, and clusters. This
dependence is a feature when the landmarks encode hypotheses of interest, but it also creates risks. Landmarks inferred
from the same gene families can introduce circularity, and a basis covering one sample may represent future families
poorly. Informed and random references produced different loading patterns in the archived analyses, but those examples
do not identify an optimal selection rule. Landmark stability, redundancy, and out-of-sample coverage should be treated
as model-selection questions.

Several limitations arise from the dissimilarities. The present implementation discards branch lengths, and three
scores ignore the reference root. Reconciliation scores depend on the species mapping and on minimisation over gene-tree
rootings. The extended-species-tree construction permits multi-copy comparisons but can be difficult to interpret for
large copy-number imbalances. Hdist and the approximate SPR score require independent correctness and sensitivity
benchmarks. Finally, rescaling does not remove all effects of gene-family size or taxon coverage, so these quantities
must be measured as possible confounders rather than assumed to have been normalised away.

Gene-tree uncertainty is another unresolved dimension. Bootstrap or posterior trees can be projected as separate rows,
but the current API does not prescribe whether a gene family should be represented by a mean signature, a distribution,
or a hierarchical model. Collapsing uncertainty to one tree may create apparent clusters driven by inference error;
treating every sampled topology as independent would instead exaggerate the effective sample size. A future extension
should retain the grouping of topologies by family and propagate within-family uncertainty into downstream analyses.

The current results are therefore evidence of feasibility, not comparative superiority. The SPR-chain example is
confounded by shared missingness within groups, the genome-level example is a single selected realisation, and the fungal
partition lacks external biological validation. A publication-ready evaluation should use repeated simulations,
predefined quantitative criteria, held-out families, simple nuisance-feature controls, and pairwise or quartet-based
baselines where their input assumptions are satisfied. Empirical clusters should be tested for stability and enrichment
against annotations that were not used to construct the signatures.

Subject to these validations, landmark representations offer a useful bridge between phylogenetic algorithms and
general statistical learning. They allow multi-view feature selection, supervised prediction, and incremental analysis
while keeping the phylogenetic meaning of the coordinates visible. The central claim is correspondingly modest: a fixed
set of species-tree landmarks and complementary dissimilarities provides a workable common feature space for gene trees
that cannot all be compared directly under standard same-leaf-set distances.
