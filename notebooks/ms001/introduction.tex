\section{Introduction}

A gene tree is an estimate of the evolutionary history of a genomic locus or gene family. Gene trees need not agree
with one another or with a species tree: incomplete lineage sorting, duplication, loss, horizontal transfer,
hybridisation, recombination, taxon sampling, model misspecification, and finite sequence information can all contribute
to discordance. The most general input is therefore a multi-copy gene family tree, in which several leaves can map to the
same species and some species can be absent.

Collections of gene trees are used both to infer species histories and to investigate heterogeneity across the genome.
Coalescent summary methods such as ASTRAL and ASTRID were originally formulated for single-copy inputs
\citep{astral,astrid}. More recent methods explicitly use multi-copy gene trees. ASTRAL-Pro defines paralogy-aware quartet
similarity \citep{astral-pro}, whereas SpeciesRax estimates rooted species trees under a duplication--transfer--loss model
\citep{Morel2022SpeciesRax}. These methods demonstrate that paralogs need not be discarded, but their primary objective
is species-tree inference. Our objective is complementary: to construct a reusable coordinate representation of each
gene family tree for exploratory comparison, visualisation, clustering, or supervised learning.

Many tree-set visualisation methods begin with pairwise distances between all trees and then use multidimensional
scaling, spectral methods, or related projections \citep{Gori2016,Jombart2017,Huang2016}. Other work maps trees directly
to vectors of splits, shape descriptors, or other features
\citep{Leigh2008,Leigh2011,Susko2006,Narechania2016,Nye2011,Yoshida2015,Lewitus2015,Kendall2016,Colijn2018}.
Quartet concordance factors and simplex plots provide a further model-based summary when gene trees share taxa
\citep{Allman2022}. These approaches are valuable, but an all-pairs dissimilarity matrix must be extended when new trees
arrive, and many standard distances require compatible leaf labels. Both issues become acute for large collections of
multi-copy families with heterogeneous taxon coverage.

We instead represent a gene tree by its dissimilarities to a fixed collection of landmark trees. The landmarks are
rooted candidate species trees containing the full species set. This construction is related to landmark-based manifold
representations and centroid methods \citep{Jeon2001-qa,Park2003,Chen2011-bp,Vin_De_Silva2003-fq}: after choosing the
landmarks, any gene tree can be projected independently. More than one dissimilarity can be evaluated against each
landmark, so distinct summaries of duplication, loss, coalescence, and topological disagreement form complementary
views of the same gene tree.

The resulting vector is called a \emph{tree signature}. Two gene trees with similar signatures make similar patterns of
agreement and disagreement with the reference trees under the selected measures. This does not imply that their
histories are identical, nor does the representation by itself identify the biological cause of discordance. It provides
a common feature space in which downstream statistical methods can be applied and in which individual features remain
associated with explicit reference trees and dissimilarities.

This fixed-coordinate property distinguishes the proposal from an embedding derived only from the sample's pairwise
distance matrix. It also creates an important dependence: the geometry is conditional on the selected reference trees.
Reference selection is therefore part of the model rather than a neutral preprocessing step, and its stability must be
evaluated when signatures are used out of sample.

SpeciesRax handle paralogy, so the manuscript must not claim that modern phylogenetic methods generally require one copy

tree'' for a landmark species tree, and ``cluster'' only for a downstream statistical grouping. Avoid using gene family
and cluster interchangeably. Use ``tree signature'' consistently; ``spectral signature'' can be mistaken for a graph
Laplacian representation.

3. Potentially unsupported inference: similar signatures are expected, but are not guaranteed, for similar histories.
The embedding can collapse distinct trees or biological mechanisms. Quantify collisions/sensitivity in simulations
before strengthening that statement.

4. Gene shopping is a possible application, but selecting loci on the same data used to estimate a species tree can
introduce circularity. Reintroduce that application only with a validation design and an appropriate citation.
